About this data

The 15 largest economies by GDP. Goods only (services appear as a single figure). Products are grouped by HS chapter (the 2-digit customs code, e.g. 85 = electrical machinery and electronics) and chapters are grouped into 16 sectors.

Sources: UN Comtrade (goods trade, annual; US imports, monthly), World Bank / WITS (tariff averages, trade taxes, tax revenue, services), OECD (corporate tax rates), US Treasury (customs duties). Insights are produced by fixed rules from these numbers; they describe, they don't forecast. Russia's figures are mirror data from its partners. Each country reports its own exports and imports, and the two sides of a flow often differ by 5–10% (timing, freight and insurance, re-exports through Hong Kong or the Netherlands).

Unemployment, measured the same way everywhere

Governments can lower their headline unemployment rate without creating a single job: count anyone who worked one hour as employed, survey only cities, drop people who stopped searching, change the survey, or stop publishing a bad number. This tab therefore uses one yardstick for every country, the ILO harmonised unemployment rate (same ILO definition, same model), and sets each country's own survey figure, its definition changes and its hidden unemployment next to it.

How to read this tab

ILO harmonised rate: unemployed = without work, available, and actively searching, for people 15+, estimated by the International Labour Organization for every country with one model, so countries and years are comparable even when national methods change. Years after the latest estimate are ILO projections. National survey (ILO def.): the country's own labour force survey, re-processed by the ILO to the ILO definition, so it is not always the government's headline figure (e.g. Nigeria's old 33% rate or China's registered rate never appear here). Coverage limits (cities only, age limits) are shown where they apply; governments' own redefinitions are listed in the watchlist. Broad (LU4): unemployed + people working fewer hours than they want + people who want work but are not searching or not available. Participation: share of people 15+ who work or look for work; a falling rate can hide unemployment.

Comparability grade (High / Medium / Low) adds penalties for: survey covering only part of the country, non-standard age or population coverage, a gap of 2 pp or more against the harmonised rate, no national data in the last 3 years, definition or method changes in the last 10 years (more for 3+), and official redefinitions or suspended publication. Minimum wage: statutory monthly minimum (ILO); USD at market rates, PPP adjusted for local prices; "% of avg wage" compares it with average monthly earnings of employees in the same year where available. Countries without a statutory minimum (wages set by collective agreements) do not appear. Data: ILOSTAT, plus hand-checked notes from national statistics offices.

About this model

IS-LM shows the interest rate (r) and output (Y) where the goods market (IS: spending falls as r rises) and the money market (LM: set by the central bank) are both in balance. When a bank raises rates, LM shifts up/left, so r rises and Y falls. When it cuts, LM shifts down/right. In an open economy (Mundell-Fleming), a higher rate than abroad attracts capital and tends to strengthen the currency, which lowers import prices and inflation. Fiscal policy moves IS: a bigger deficit (stimulus) shifts it right, consolidation shifts it left. Energy shocks hit both sides: dearer oil raises costs and inflation everywhere, shifts IS left for energy importers (lost real income) and right for exporters (windfall).

Rules used here: stance = 12-month change in the policy rate (±0.25 pp). Policy is restrictive when the real rate (policy rate − CPI inflation) is more than 0.5 pp above an assumed neutral real rate, and accommodative when it is more than 0.5 pp below. Fiscal impulse = this year's rise in the government deficit (% of GDP); ±0.3 pp or more counts as expansionary/contractionary. Energy: 12-month change in Brent oil × the country's net energy-import share; +10% oil ≈ +0.3 pp headline inflation. Inflation outlook combines the real-rate position, the stance, a ≥3% currency move, the fiscal impulse and any ≥15% oil move (halved if the EIA projects prices to fall). These are rule-based estimates, not forecasts. Data: BIS (policy rates, CPI, exchange rates), OECD Economic Outlook and IMF World Economic Outlook (budgets, debt), EIA (oil price and projection), World Bank (energy imports).
